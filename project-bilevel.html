Simulation-Based Optimization of Battery and Microgrid Dispatch for Factories

Joint production scheduling and energy dispatch · First-author manuscript, major revision at IISE Transactions

13–25% lower electricity cost than a rule-based controller across five production scenarios, in simulation.

Problem: coordinate energy demand and supply

Production schedules determine when a factory needs electricity. Solar and wind vary over time, while storage and time-of-use prices affect how that demand should be supplied. Optimizing either decision alone can miss the benefit of coordinating them.

What I built

  • A constrained simulator: a Python microgrid environment with solar, wind, battery storage, and grid power, using real inputs from three locations; SOC limits, efficiency losses, and time-of-use tariffs are modeled explicitly.
  • Joint scheduling and dispatch: a genetic algorithm searches job sequences and start times; PPO allocates energy; a neural surrogate estimates dispatch cost to accelerate the scheduling search. Statistical time-series forecasts supply renewable-generation information.
  • Reliable decision design: a factory-first action space prevents selling renewables and then buying grid power to supply the same factory demand. I checked surrogate rankings and corrected terminal battery-energy valuation.

Why the schedule matters

With renewable supply and the RBC dispatcher held fixed, changing the job schedule changes demand and energy cost. The joint approach considers renewable availability, storage, and time-of-use prices together—including charging at lower prices and using stored energy during higher-price periods.

Results and validation

Electricity cost was 13–25% lower than a rule-based controller across five production scenarios. Component ablations were evaluated with F- and t-tests; MPC comparisons covered seven planning horizons and 1,350 cluster runs. The headline percentage is versus the rule-based controller, not MPC.

Connection to EV charging operations

Trip assignments and departure times shape charging demand; renewable generation, prices, and charger capacity shape supply. The same coupled decision structure could coordinate trips and charging while preserving departure readiness. Vehicle connection windows, departure-energy requirements, and charger limits would be additional modeling requirements.

The implemented results are factory microgrid simulations; the EV application is a proposed extension. Related work: RAM-DQN and time-window routing.

Technical detail: reward shaping

Charging incurs an immediate cost but may reduce purchases at a later peak price. I used potential-based reward shaping to make that delayed value more informative during training, alongside a penalty for renewable waste.

Total reward
rt = −Ct + F(st, st+1) − Wpent
Potential difference
F(st, st+1) = Φ(st+1) − Φ(st)
Value of stored energy
Φ(st) = (SOCt / Bcap) · varbt · β
Price-spread signal
varbt = ρmax + η max(0, ρmax − ρt)

Ct: grid purchase cost − renewable export revenue + battery cycling cost. ρt: current buy price; ρmax = maxτ≥t ρτ from the known ToU schedule. Bcap: battery capacity; β, η: calibration coefficients.

Renewable-waste penalty
Wpent = κ ρt max(0, Rt − Dt(σ,δ) − Broom,t)

Rt is solar + wind generation; Dt(σ,δ) is schedule-dependent demand; Broom,t is available battery headroom. The penalty encourages preserving room for renewable energy.
